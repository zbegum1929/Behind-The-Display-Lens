HOW SMART GLASSES TURN LIGHT INTO INFORMATION

Light Behind the Lens

A tiny light source, a transparent lens, and a carefully designed optical path. These are some of the key elements that allow smart glasses to place digital information in front of your eyes without completely blocking the world around you.

Unlike a conventional screen, an optical see-through augmented reality (AR) display does not simply show an image on a surface. It uses a combination of light generation, optical guidance, and controlled redirection to deliver an image to the viewer's eye.

But how does light travel from the side of a pair of glasses to the eye?

01

The Light Source: Where the Image Begins

The process begins inside the temple of the glasses, near the hinge connecting the frame to the lens.

A compact display system, commonly called a light engine, generates the light that carries the digital image. Depending on the device architecture, this system may use technologies such as micro-OLED, micro-LED, LCoS, or laser-based projection.

The light engine produces the image, while optical components help shape and direct the emitted light toward the lens.

This distinction is important: the digital information itself does not travel through the glass. Instead, the light carrying the image is guided through the optical system.

The goal is to keep the projection system small enough to fit into an everyday glasses frame while delivering sufficient brightness and image quality.

02

Entering the Waveguide: A Path Inside the Lens

Once the image light leaves the projection optics, it reaches an optical element called the in-coupler.

The in-coupler directs the incoming light into a thin, transparent optical layer known as a waveguide.

Waveguides can be made from carefully selected glass or optical polymers. Their refractive properties allow light to remain guided within the material under suitable conditions.

This is the beginning of the optical journey through the lens.

03

Total Internal Reflection: Keeping Light on Track

One of the most important physical principles behind many AR waveguide displays is Total Internal Reflection (TIR).

When light travels inside a material with a higher refractive index and reaches a boundary with a lower refractive index at an angle greater than the critical angle, it can be reflected back into the original material rather than passing through the boundary.

This process allows light to travel along a thin waveguide through repeated internal reflections.

Instead of requiring a bulky projector directly in front of the eye, the waveguide can guide image-bearing light across the lens while preserving a relatively transparent appearance.

The light is not simply bouncing randomly. Its direction is determined by the geometry, refractive properties, and optical coupling structures of the system.

04

The Out-Coupler: Directing Light Toward the Eye

After travelling through the waveguide, the image light reaches another optical structure called the out-coupler.

Its purpose is to redirect guided light out of the waveguide and toward the viewer's eye.

Depending on the optical architecture, this may involve partially reflective surfaces, diffraction gratings, or other specially designed optical structures.

Some waveguide systems also replicate the light beam at multiple locations. This technique, known as exit pupil expansion, helps create a larger viewing region, called the eyebox.

The eyebox is the area within which the viewer can position their eye and still see the projected image.

Without a sufficiently large eyebox, even a small movement of the glasses could make the digital image disappear.

05

Seeing Two Worlds at Once

The final stage takes place at the eye.

The out-coupled image light enters the pupil and is focused by the eye's optical system onto the retina. At the same time, ambient light from the real environment passes through the transparent lens.

The visual system therefore receives two sources of light:

  • Light from the physical environment.
  • Image-bearing light from the AR display.

The result is an optical see-through experience in which digital information can appear overlaid on the user's surroundings.

The challenge is to balance image brightness, transparency, contrast, colour accuracy, and optical efficiency without making the glasses unnecessarily thick or heavy.

06

Follow the Light: An Interactive Optical Journey

Our animation illustrates the main stages of this process.

  1. Light Generation — The light engine inside the glasses' temple produces image-bearing light. The animated source represents the beginning of the optical path.
  2. Optical Coupling — The light reaches the in-coupler near the lens and enters the waveguide. Its direction changes as it is coupled into the optical layer.
  3. Internal Reflection — The light travels through the waveguide by total internal reflection. The repeated changes in direction represent how the optical structure guides the light across the lens.
  4. Light Extraction — At the out-coupler, the guided light is redirected out of the lens and toward the eye.
  5. Image Perception — The eye receives the image-bearing light, allowing the user to perceive digital information while still seeing the surrounding physical environment.

The animation is a simplified educational representation. Real waveguide displays use carefully engineered optical structures, and their exact internal geometry varies between technologies.

07

The Science Behind the Design

Designing a waveguide display involves more than simply directing light toward an eye. Several optical parameters must work together.

Optical conceptWhy it matters
Refractive indexDetermines how light behaves when passing between materials
Critical angleDefines the condition for total internal reflection
In-coupling efficiencyDetermines how effectively light enters the waveguide
Out-coupling efficiencyDetermines how effectively light reaches the viewing region
EyeboxDefines the range of eye positions from which the image remains visible
Field of View (FoV)Determines the angular extent of the displayed image
Optical uniformityHelps maintain consistent brightness across the viewing region

These parameters are interconnected. For example, increasing the size of the eyebox can introduce challenges in brightness uniformity and optical efficiency.

This is why waveguide design remains an active area of optical engineering research.

08

Why Does This Technology Matter?

Waveguide-based AR displays offer a way to integrate digital information into lightweight, glasses-like devices.

Instead of looking down at a phone or holding a separate screen, users can access selected information within their field of view.

However, making this technology practical requires solving several challenges: improving brightness in daylight, reducing power consumption, maintaining colour accuracy, expanding the eyebox, and preserving the transparency of the lens.

The future of smart glasses depends not only on making displays smaller, but also on controlling light with greater precision.

A digital image may begin inside the frame, but its journey ends in the eye.
